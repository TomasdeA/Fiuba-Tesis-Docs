People who are blind or have low vision face significant difficulties when navigating unfamiliar or
dynamic environments. Traditional assistive tools such as the white cane have limited range and
spatial resolution, and fail to effectively detect obstacles at head height. Meanwhile, fields such
as mobile robotics have consolidated a set of perception, mapping and inter-process communication
technologies that enable autonomous systems to interpret their surroundings in real time, yet
these technologies are seldom applied to the problem of personal assisted mobility.

The main focus of this thesis is to study, evaluate, and adapt existing technologies, including
active stereo depth sensing, probabilistic occupancy grids, inertial data fusion, and the ROS 2
ecosystem. The contribution does not lie in the creation of novel techniques, but rather in
determining which of these technologies are most suitable and how to integrate them to build a
functional, low-cost assistive device, while considering the constraints of a portable system:
limited embedded computing resources and low latency. The choice of the haptic channel as the
output interface is grounded in the principle of sensory substitution: the brain can reorganize to
interpret tactile stimuli as spatial information when vision is reduced or absent. On that basis,
the broader project seeks to translate visual spatial information into haptic stimuli interpretable
for navigation; it is structured in two complementary stages: (i)~environment capture and local
mapping, and (ii)~translation into a haptic interface. This thesis addresses exclusively the first
stage.

Specifically, a ROS 2 module is implemented to transform measurements from an Intel RealSense
D435i camera into a compact representation of the immediate surroundings. The configuration
includes head mounting of the camera and deployment on Raspberry Pi~5, with parameters to adjust
acquisition and processing load. The development follows three incremental
versions. The v0 version prioritizes a direct transformation with few processing stages: it reduces the depth image to
a grid of 5 rows by 10 columns, converts it to haptic intensities, and generates the UART frame
intended for the physical interface of the project's second stage, while also providing a visualization of the results for
debugging purposes.
Building on this basis, v1 improves measurement interpretation: it aligns the point cloud with
gravity, removes ground points and an excluded upper region, and groups obstacles by direction
and height. Finally, v2 adds spatial memory by using the filtered output of v1
to build and maintain a 2D local occupancy map based on log-odds, with Bresenham ray casting,
a sliding window and RGB-D odometry assisted by inertial orientation, preserving recent obstacle
information even after obstacles leave the camera's field of
view. A fusion node combines this spatial memory with the instantaneous frontal perception of v1.

The system evaluation covers depth error, end-to-end latency, obstacle detection precision and
recall, temporal stability of the local map, and resource consumption. The analysis compares
performance on the development PC and Raspberry Pi~5 and examines operating profiles and their
restrictions.
